## 0. Summary and decisions to validate

**The answer the lab gives.** Yes, you can store energy by lifting weights, and the physics is perfectly honest: 1 kWh is one tonne raised 367 m.
That same sentence is why it is hard: at the scale of a farm, a 20-tonne weight in a 28 m tower holds about 1 kWh, and a 40 kg battery module holds
4.4 times that. The machine is cheap to imagine and expensive to hold up. What gravity offers in return is power that does not depend on the energy,
no chemical wear, and a mass that can be anything heavy. The lab shows both sides with the numbers, and it shows where real projects stand today.

**The machine (proposed).** A **generic small-site store on a farm**: a 20 t weight (concrete blocks on a guided carrier), a 20 m stroke, a steel
lattice tower of about 28 m, a four-fall wire rope, a stationary electric hoist with a variable-speed 15 kW motor run in both directions, a regenerative
drive on the farm's 400 V supply. **Gross energy 1.09 kWh** (`derived`, K7). It is built around one published hoist table and one published block,
and it is the same size in energy as the only such machine built in Europe (Leith, Edinburgh, 2 × 25 t, 15 m tower: 1.1 to 2 kWh, K17). It is never
presented as anyone's product. **[C8, §11]** One stroke delivers **780 Wh** by the model (658 to 878), not the 0.8 to 0.9 kWh first estimated (K52).

**Four findings that change the roadmap's entry (§5.7).**

1. **The TEDx talk is not about gravity.** "Une solution béton" (S3) is a concrete **flywheel** spinning at nearly 1,000 km/h at the rim, in a vacuum: kinetic energy, with
   nothing lifted (S3 3:05, 5:01, 5:36). It gives no figure for this lab. Recommendation: drop it as a source (Q3). The roadmap's title was inferred from the name. **[C9, §11]** Done: the talk is out.
2. **The Globe Oculaire video (S2) has checkable errors**: it places Gravitricity in Germany (1:58, the company is Scottish), and says a 35 t block raised 100 m holds "9.6 MJ"
   (8:47); the true figure is 34.3 MJ, or 9.54 kWh, so the unit is wrong. Nothing else in it is sourced. It is not used for validation.
3. **The field has moved since the sceptical videos, and not in gravity's favour.** Gravitricity Ltd is in creditors' voluntary liquidation since 1 October 2025 with about
   £7,914 of assets (S16). **gravitricity.com now serves a casino affiliate page** (fetched 2/11/2026): do not link it. Rudong (25 MW / 100 MWh) is tested and grid-connected but
   was still awaiting final approvals in March 2026, and a secondary source says no gravity plant anywhere is in commercial operation (S17). The lab must say so, with dates.
4. **"Nouveau : presque rien" is optimistic for the physics.** E = mgh is one line, but the real lessons (losses at part load, the speed below which the machine gives
   nothing, the weight that will not come down on its own) need a small loss model that neither `sim/electric` nor `sim/mech` has (§5).

### Sanity checks of the sources

- **S2.** "35 t, 100 m, 9.6 MJ" (8:47): the correct value is 34.3 MJ = 9.54 kWh. It appears to confuse MJ and kWh. "150 m, 5,000 t" (9:00) is 2.04 MWh: small, and said to "stabilise a wind farm". Gravitricity in Germany (1:58): wrong. "2 to 3 M$/MW" (7:02) has no source, and S8 gives $1.44 M/MW for 100 MW at 4 h. Swiss "cable break" test (9:43): unsourced, not used. **S2 fails: not a validation source.**
- **S4.** 10,000 × 20 m³ × 0.41 t/m³ = 82,000 t ✓. (arithmetic, on a stated guess). 3 MW × 25 % × 24 h = 18 MWh ✓. 35 MWh agrees with S19's goal of 7,000 blocks, which needs a mean drop of 52 m (`derived`) ✓, plausible. Its 200,000 m³ of concrete is about twice what S19's 7,000 blocks of 35 t need (102,000 m³): the CO₂ is an upper figure. It predates Rudong.
- **S5.** 20 t × 10 m/s² × 10 m = 2 MJ ✓, on guessed mass and height: K21 gives about 3.9 kWh per block, 7 times more, so its "half an hour of kettle per block" undersells the tower, while its point about having only a handful of blocks is not contradicted. Water tower 12,000 m³ × 50 m: 1.6 MWh exact, it says "about a thousand kWh" ✓ roughly. "Same energy per tonne, water or concrete" (12:36) ✓ and it is the sharpest point of the video. Crane limit said as 20 mph and "10 m/s at 30 mph" (4:28–4:40): those three do not agree (8.9, 10, 13.4 m/s) and the source is a quoted clip. **check**.
- **S1.** 16,000 / 4 = 4,000 ✓. 3³ = 27 ✓. 620 rpm to at most 4 rpm needs 155: five stages (243) fit, four (81) do not; the video does not say (`derived`). The stall at 12 kg is real physics (K50); the numbers on power are absent.
- **K21.** Up 95 %, down 79.5 %: a strange asymmetry for a machine that uses the same chain both ways. Perhaps the block is lighter than taken, or the lift includes something else. The lab states the 75 % and does not build on the split.
- **S6.** 10 MWh at 100 m with $80/t: 36,697 t × 80 / 10,000 kWh = $294/kWh ✓ (table 6 reproduced).
- **K33 and K34** ✓, and 20 GWh / 900 MW = about 22 h at full generating power.
- **Nothing in any source measures a machine this small.** K47 to K53 are the model's own, and the lab says so once.

## 5. Physics (for `lab-physicist`)

**State.** Height z, speed v, mode (charge, discharge, hold, trip), brake state, LFP state of charge, PV and load profiles. Pure TypeScript in `src/sim`, no DOM.

**Equations.**

1. **Energy.** E = m g z, with K1 as a test. Rope mass counted as S6 eq. 15 does; here about 0.7 % (K14). **[C2, §11]** That is its mass; in energy, 0.36 %.
2. **Reeving.** n = 4 falls: rope speed = n·v; tension = m(g + a) / (n·η_r), with η_r different up and down; per-sheave efficiency **unknown**, take 0.98 as an `assumption` and note that EN 13001-3-2 §5.2.3 has the formula, not read. S6 adds friction as 2.5 % of the conveyance and 10 % of the rope mass, a usable alternative.
3. **Drum and gearbox.** ω_d = n·v/r_d, τ_d = T·r_d; gearbox through `driveTrain()`, with drag and mesh losses and an asymmetry between driving and back-driven. A static friction term gives K50.
4. **Motor.** Torque follows the weight, not the speed. Copper loss R·(T/k)², iron loss with speed, plus fan and brake coil, fitted to the **rated point K10**. That fit is a parameterisation of a data sheet: state it, and keep it out of any tuning towards K21 or K35.
5. **Drive and supply.** Fixed plus current-squared losses, `assumption`, flagged. Cables with `cableResistance()`.
6. **Dynamics.** a = v/τ (S7 eq. 12), peak power S7 eq. 13–14. Check S6's example: v = 0.28 m/s, a = 0.5 m/s² gives 0.56 s.
7. **Control.** Charge: v follows PV surplus, between K8's limits. Discharge: follows the load, stops below K49, or runs in **bursts into the LFP buffer**. Grid loss: chopper and resistor, then brakes. Overspeed: governor, safety gear.
8. **Comparators.** LFP from K31 and `ocv()`; pumped from K33 and K34; tower ladder from K7 to K25. Three sides of one table, each number tagged.
9. **Carbon and money.** Bars with ranges (K37, K38, K45, K46), shown as orders of magnitude only. Never a false precision.

## 7. Safety, law, credits

**Hazards.** A suspended load of 20 t. Rope failure, which can come with little visible warning if inspection is skipped. Brake failure; two brakes with independent paths. Overspeed. Falling blocks. Wind on a 28 m lattice. Lightning. 400 V AC and a DC link of about 600 V (`estimate`) that stays charged. Regeneration into a dead grid. Working at height. Crushing at the buffer. S1 shows the failure honestly: 68 kg was too much, a cover without screws dropped the load (18:58), and the concrete below cracked.

**The warning, said once, in the `tower` world where the risk is seen and in the Sources panel.** "This machine is a teaching reconstruction: never stand under a suspended load, and never build one without a structural engineer and a lifting inspector. Even a home-sized version can kill." In the spoken guide, one sentence at `hoist-rope`, per principle D.
